The Second Naval Race was the great capital-ship competition that followed the end of the Weltkrieg and preceded the collapse of the old British world order. Between 1919 and 1925, Britain, Germany, the United States, and Japan entered a renewed struggle over battleships, battlecruisers, aircraft carriers, cruisers, naval bases, and maritime prestige.

Its center lay in the North Sea, where the Royal Navy and the Kaiserliche Marine continued to watch each other after the peace, but its consequences reached far beyond Europe. In Washington D.C., the United States measured its future battle fleet against both the Atlantic powers and Japan. In Tokyo, the Japanese Empire watched western program with a fearful vision, seeing it as a imminent threat to Japan’s position in East Asia. By 1925, the race had drawn in every major shipbuilding power until the British Revolution brought it to an abrupt end, leaving unfinished ships, divided fleets, and revised doctrines across the world.

1919: The Peace in the Shadow

The peace of 1919 ended the land war in Europe, yet it failed to settle the naval question. Germany had defeated France and secured its continental position, while Britain escaped the harsh terms that fell upon its allies. The British Empire remained formally intact, and the Royal Navy preserved the prestige of a fleet that had contained Germany throughout the war, but the miserable memory of U-boat blockade still left a deep scar on the nation. German officers, politicians, and industrialists, however, also interpreted that outcome with mixed feelings. Although Germany had won the war, famines, shortages, and specifically the incompetence of its sea power displayed during the war, had made the threat from the Royal Navy ever so concrete.

During the final phase of the war, Germany had already committed itself to a new generation of capital ships. The Mackensen and Ersatz Yorck battlecruisers had been laid down under wartime pressure in 1915 and 1916. Later battleship studies promised guns, machinery, and protection far beyond the ships that had fought at Jutland, with two of them laid just a few months before the peace. These hulls became the first practical problem of the postwar debate in a Germany tangled by burden of reconstruction.

The Reichstag did not accept the naval program automatically. The Ludendorff Crisis of 1920 weakened the authority of the old military dictatorship and reopened questions that had been suppressed during the war. Deputies emphasized demobilization and political reform over naval expansion, putting shipbuilding industrialists and naval officers at minority. The SPD (Sozialdemokratische Partei Deutschlands) was in the middle of the parliamentary storm, who argued about cancellation of wartime battleships at a politically delicate moment, and that completion would require further appropriations during a period of fiscal strain. Acting as political allies of the SPD, liberals and state representatives asked why a victorious Germany needed to continue the most expensive branch of armaments when France had collapsed and Russia had been removed from the eastern balance.

Within the Kaiserliche Marine, its leading institutions had little desire to surrender the wartime program. The Admiralstab led by Adolf von Trotha and Paul Behncke studied the operational lessons of Jutland and the blockade, and publicly argued that the German victory would remain incomplete unless it possessed a fleet capable of preventing a future blockade. They rallied around Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz, who also had a strong political ally outside the Reichstag, the Kaiser.

Before any political compromise could be reached, Britain provided that argument. As Admiral Sir David Beatty took office as the First Sea Lord in late-1919, the Admiralty, led by Beatty and a generation of officers shaped by the Grand Fleet and the blockade, refused to reduce the fleet to a purely peacetime establishment. Beatty and the Board of Admiralty accepted budget pressure, but they would not allow Britain’s heavy-ship margin to disappear while new German battleships remained in drydocks. The decommission schedule made by the Treasury was postponed, and fast capital ships, particularly the Admiral-class, received high priority for completion under Beatty’s request, which it became a political signal that Britain intended to preserve a fleet capable of meeting Germany in home waters.

1920: Chain of Suspicion

The German debate changed once British policy became clear. A shipbuilding program that had looked vulnerable in 1919 became easier to defend in 1920, only this time, naval deputies no longer emphasized sunk cost, but a pratical threat posed by a much stronger enemy that was gaining strength. Even though the SPD circle was still against it, the 1920 Flottengesetze (Fleet Law) was narrowly passed as conservatives switched side, and it therefore continued the completion of wartime battleships. Before that, the two Preußen-class battleships laid in the last year just inched toward completion, but their construction accelerated once millions of Deutsch Marks were poured into the industry.

German industrial machine began running at full blast in the latter half of 1920, perfectly on time to undertake unemployment from the demobilization, which it also reduced objection in politics. This situation, however, was a nightmare for the British Admiralty. Despite strong opposition from the Treasury, Beatty’s appeal to continue Britain’s own post-war building program was approved to maintain the superiority of Royal Navy under the erosion of the Two-Power Standard.

Across the Atlantic, the United States Navy studied the same developments through the General Board with a watchful eye in Washington. Despite neutrality in the Weltkrieg, American naval leadership had learned technical lessons from many sea battles as attache or observers. New machines, doctrines, and long-term economic and psychological impacts by blockades all deepened their concern about the scale of the next war. A common sense at that time was: even though America was protected by distance of the Pacific and Atlantic Ocean, it still needed a renewed and large fleet to protect its growing interests globally. A fleet able to defend one ocean could leave the other exposed, and continuation of the Big Navy Act was considered a way to seal it. The South Dakota-class battleships and Lexington-class battlecruisers were successively laid according to the plan. By optimistic viewpoint of the General Board, completion of these ships would give the United States an upper hand in an upcoming naval race.

In comparison to western countries, Japan was almost worn out by years of naval expansion and was still at war with Germany. The Imperial Japanese Navy had occupied German Pacific possessions and expected diplomatic reward for its effort, only to found the collapse of Entente powers. British and American unwillingness to support Japan’s claim to Tsingtao sharpened the sense that Japan could not rely on goodwill from London, Berlin, or Washington. The naval expenditure, however, formed a practical constraint on Japan’s foreign policy when it could no longer keep up with rivals’. Many planners knew it from the beginning that it was a race that Japan could neither win nor afford, but the fear about future fate of the nation forced them to make a hard decision.

Thus, by the end of 1920, the race had already acquired its structure, involving Germany, Britain, the United States, and Japan, all of them built on a shaking political and economic ground.

1921: The Monster of Devonport

The year 1921 shifted the naval race from a North Sea problem into a global one.

With Germany’s battleship construction proceeding, the Admiralty had put heavy pressure on the Director of Naval Construction Eustace Tennyson d’Enycourt for next-generation capital ships. His answers were N3 battleship and G3 battlecruiser with unified gun-before-engine layout. Although the designing was mostly completed during the war, it took time for Britain’s post-war shipbuilding industry to adapt to new capital ships with unprecedented scales. The G3 was not a mere copy of old battlecruisers, it carried nine 16-in guns and sailed at over 30kn, with specific improvements on armor scheme that outmatched many dreadnoughts. The N3 had even more astounding characteristics of nine 18-in guns and the thickest armor ever manufactured. Both of them adopted all-or-nothing scheme, concentrating armor and armament around compact citadels, which promoted their protection of core buoyancy to a level beyond foreign estimates.

The initial plan was to build four N3s in 1921 and four G3s in 1922, but it was proved to be unaffordable. The plan was then changed to four batches of eight ships, one pair each year from 1921 to 1924.

On 22 July 1921, the first ship of N3-class, HMS Magnificent, was laid in Devonport, followed by HMS Majestic in Clydebank three months later. HMS Magnificent caused an earthquake in navies of the world, just like HMS Dreadnought in 1905, culminating in movements within multiple naval architecture departments in participants of the naval race. The German Admiralstab received this disturbing news from London, casting shadows over the once optimistic attitude over fleet issues. The monster of Devonport, as many German admirals might call it, had evoked insecurity in Berlin as it rendered German variants completely incompetent. The work on Scharnhorst (Ersatz Yorck) and Preußen continued, yet an internal planning on response to HMS Magnificent had been initiated.

On the other side of the world, Tsingtao Accord had officially ended the unresolved state of war in East Asia and returned the prewar territorial balance in the German-leased Chinese territories, while recognizing Japan’s place as an object of a truce deal instead of a loser in the war. This settlement reduced the immediate diplomatic crisis between Germany and Japan, yet it deeply shaken Japanese glory established since the Meiji Restoration.

In Tokyo, naval officers and civilian government started to realize just how fragile past Japanese victories could be in the face of western powers with stronger industrial base and naval forces. Public statements emphasized order and international cooperation for decency, usually depicted as a vague Japanese victory, while private memoranda within the Navy Ministry and Navy General Staff treated the settlement as defeat rather than losing face. To admirals who had been through Tsushima, Japan had fought in the Pacific, taken German positions, and then watched those gains narrowed by diplomatic pressure. The Navy’s answer was the reinforcement of the Eight-Eight Fleet concept, which tactically assumed that Japan must be able to impose a decision in western Pacific waters before richer powers could bring their full resources to bear, and politically, utilizing the fleet as confidence for diplomacy.

Japanese construction during this period reflected that ambition. It moved to Tosa-class battleships and Amagi-class battlecruisers after its battle line took shape with commission of Nagato and Mutsu. Compared to western battleships in service, these ships promised greater protection, heavier armaments, and faster speed, marking the unparalleled Japanese quality. As naval constructors at Kure and Yokosuka learned to manage larger hulls and increasingly complex machinery, the Navy’s technical community had to admit Japan’s advantage would not last long before it was overwhelmed by western powers with much stronger industrial base.

British program was received with an even harder feeling in Washington. All estimates made by planners who proposed the Big Navy Act collapsed overnight when they learned about the stats, rendering 10-year effort on standard battleships in vain. Although South Dakota and Lexington were representing a deviation from the standard-type strategy, the General Board didn’t predict the radical reactions from other naval powers, and it therefore resulted in anxiety among American officers who had watched developments in Europe closely. Responses from Germany and Japan remained uncertain, yet the General Board made a bold and swift decision to abandon the standard-type strategy, a doctrine that shaped the USN of today, and proposed an amendment to the Act, cancelling ships not started of the original 1916 plan and financing bigger ships to match British design.

As the invisible war went on inside designing rooms, heavy guns became the common language of the year of 1921. Although it was not announced, the Second Naval Race had expanded beyond the question of whether Germany would complete wartime ships, but a global naval maneuver escalating every day.

1922: Battleship Frenzy

The decisive change in 1922 was bureaucratic when naval competition moved from crisis memoranda into regular programs. Ship silhouette manuals renewed monthly as more ships, capital and escort, were pumped out of dockyards. Gun mountings entered procurement schedules, and dockyard improvements appeared beside welfare expenditures and debt service in national budgets. The race became harder to stop once naval plans had become annual administrative routine.

After a 2-year extension, the Admiralty had finally caved in facing furious Treasury on the issue of disbanding the Grand Fleet, also due to estimation of a smaller but more advanced Royal Navy under construction in dockyards. For the time being, this decision relieved the pressure on fiscal when both reconstruction and naval expansion were literally burning millions of pounds. As planned, two Nelson-class battlecruisers, derived from design G3, were laid on slipways in Scotland. These designs also showed how much British naval thought had changed since the first dreadnought race, which the fleet still valued speed and gun power, although underwater protection, deck armor, director fire control, and aircraft spotting now received serious weight in design discussions.

The political argument in London grew sharper as the Great Slump deepened. Clydeside, the Tyne, Barrow, Sheffield, South Wales, and the royal dockyards all felt the contraction of postwar industry. Admiralty officials argued that shipbuilding preserved skilled labor and strategic capacity. Critics in Parliament asked why unemployed workers should accept wage pressure while the state paid for larger battleships. The labor movement increasingly treated naval expenditure as evidence that the ruling class would fund imperial arms before domestic reconstruction. The Admiralty’s defense rested on Germany. Every report from Kiel, Hamburg, or Wilhelmshaven strengthened its position.

German naval planning in 1922 took a similar institutional form. The Reichsmarineamt secured continuation funds for Scharnhorst-class battlecruiser completion and heavy-ship studies to match HMS Magnificent, yet it was hard for Germany to immediately catch up with a much limited shipbuilding industry. Negotiations and researches were conducted by the navy and ThyssenKrupp to assess the tolerance of German industry to build a 50,000-ton ship. The institutional resistance came from the Reichstag which continued to argue over cost, yet parliamentary debates only made cancellation harder as ships advanced toward launch.

The United States, however, maintained a different rhythm free from reconstruction. American industrial capacity allowed ambitious designs to survive even when appropriations slowed. Facing evolving ship variants across the Atlantic, many officers had regarded the fast but poorly armored battlecruiser as a failure. Others pointed to aircraft experiments and suggested that large, fast hulls could serve aviation. Out of six planned, two got cancelled, and another pair of them were rearranged for carrier conversion.

Besides doctrinal disputes, American politics also restrained the pace. President McAdoo and Congress faced public pressure to reduce spending and keeping economic prosperity. Naval advocates framed their case in European and Japanese threats theory, but it was obvious that Congress wouldn’t allow the amendment to the Big Navy Act to escalate again, forcing the navy to reconsider on-going battleship projects. USS Iowa and USS Massachusetts were suddenly under fire, and soon removed from the building list to compensate an even larger project.

1923: Beginning of the End

By early-1923, the race had reached its peak. The major navies had committed themselves to large programs, then discovered that no program survived first contact with budgets, technology, and intelligence reports. Economic ministries and treasuries tried to slow commitments already blessed by naval staffs, and warned the economy was at stake.

Germany gained momentum during this year. The first postwar battleship was launched, giving the High Seas Fleet a modern fast wing, and helped the navy answer critics who had mocked expensive hulls as unfinished monuments to wartime ambition, but the fundamental question of Magnificent equivalent remained not answered.

Even though these ships were nowhere close to British counterparts, the British Admiralty intelligence took these German completions seriously, defending the necessity of G3 and N3 programs. British constructors emphasized that future ships must survive long-range fire and torpedo damage, because the next fleet action would be fought with better optics, aircraft spotting, and heavier shells than Jutland had seen. Beatty still held his office with an iron fist, but even a radical naval advocate like him could not neglect the actual harm brought by massive ship programs on civilian economy. Surprisingly, he agreed to postpone the timetable for later ships. Over the year, only one Magnificent-class battleships was laid, and the schedule was to lay one battlecruiser in 1924, and a pair of battleship and battlecruiser in 1925.

The same year also exposed Britain’s weakness. The Great Slump had become a structural crisis with falling wages and unemployment feeding radical politics in shipyards and mining districts. Naval construction preserved employment in certain areas, yet it did not restore broader prosperity. Many British people still suffered heavily from the trauma of war, and it had brought the Admiralty to the center of public opinion. The House were filled with angry deputies yelling “No more new ships”, a trend that further divided British society.

1923 brought renewed attention to the problem of scale in America. Reports of British and German heavy designs strengthened the General Board’s argument for the Louisiana-class battleships, an 18-inch-gun ship design that answered more than any single foreign design. It was a statement that the United States could outbuild the competition if its political system released the funds. The Bureau of Construction and Repair had to address displacement, hull strength, underwater protection, and the enormous machinery demands of ships intended for oceanic operations. By November, the preparation for the first member of the Louisiana-class were ready.

The Massachusetts question also developed further. Once a battleship, her speed restrained the potential of carrier conversion. The Bureau of Aeronautics, newly important within the Navy Department, began to argue that the fleet’s future depended on the air. Its chief, Rear Admiral William Moffet, joined by innovative officers such as Joseph Reeves, supported carriers because they strengthened the battle fleet rather than replacing it, and pushed the conversion to happen. This made aviation easier to defend inside a Navy still culturally attached to gunnery, and reshaped American naval officers’ thoughts in a world obsessed with big guns.

Japan adjusted under pressure. The Kii program moved forward as the next stage of the Eight-Eight Fleet, while the Navy General Staff studied how to defeat an American advance across the Pacific. The answer increasingly combined capital ships with attritional doctrine. Submarines would scout and weaken the enemy, while cruisers and destroyers would deliver torpedo attacks, preferably at night. The battle fleet would then engage under conditions chosen by Japan. This doctrine was called “Kantai Kessen”, and it allowed the Navy to defend quality and training even when fiscal limits threatened the number of ships.

Technological change made 1923 a turning point. Even at the peak of battleship frenzy when nobody dared to pause facing stronger capital ships, new thoughts were emerging from young officers who saw values on those crude flight decks.

1924: The Great Collapse

The naval race entered open political crisis in 1924. The first domino fell in Britain and started a chain reaction that shaped the world we know today. Naval ministries could still produce plans, and shipyards could still launch hulls, yet domestic stability now surfaced as the sword of Damocles.

Years after years, the Great Slump fed industrial disputes, radical organization, and distrust of the governing class. The Red Summer and the General Strike placed dockworkers, miners, railwaymen, and shipyard laborers at the center of national politics. The Royal Navy depended on those same workers for its ambition, but it was starting to feel like an illusion. Admiralty memoranda from this period increasingly treated labor unrest as a strategic issue, underscoring that a fleet could be powerful on paper while immobilized by strikes.

The Admiralty had made concessions but refused to abandon the race during the crisis, especially when Germany laid its response to HMS Magnificent after a three-year delay, the Elsaß-class battleships with eight 45cm guns. Its leaders feared that visible retreat would encourage Germany and weaken British authority across the Empire. The completion of advanced capital ships and the continuation of G3 and N3 work became acts of institutional defiance. Fleet officers wanted the Navy to remain the one instrument through which Britain could still act as a great power, but the Treasury, facing falling revenues and social demands, viewed the same ships as increasingly dangerous luxuries.

It all collapsed when Austin Chamberlain’s administration signed the bill to end naval investment and start disarmament. On 18 May 1924, the crews of HMS Hood and HMS Repulse, returning from the Imperial Tour of the Dominions, mutinied after learning of the pay cuts. Thousands of workers who were absorbed in the shipbuilding industry over the past few years lost their jobs overnight, feeding an ever growing unemployed people. Attempts to suppress the unrest across the nation only widened it. Strikes spread, syndicalist influence grew, and the Navy began purging its own ranks even as the monarchy's position collapsed ashore.

Germany, despite its exhaustion, observed Britain’s unrest with satisfaction and caution. Naval officers feared that a cornered British state might rely more heavily on the fleet to preserve imperial cohesion, and asked for pledge of continuation of the mega-battleship programs. The Reichstag’s opinion narrowed around pace and allocation rather than principle. The two sides reached an agreement after exhausting debate, cutting the fantasy of five Elsaß-class to two, which it symbolized that Germany had actually accepted naval inferiority to Britain.

Japan entered 1924 with its naval ambitions under heavier scrutiny. The Diet and Finance Ministry pressed the Navy to justify every large hull. With Tosa and Amagi draining every last piece of yen, Army leaders argued that Manchuria and China required attention no less urgent than the Pacific. Prime Minister Tanaka Giichi and his allies favored a stronger continental posture and tighter fiscal discipline, but the Navy answered through doctrine.

In April, two Kii-class battleships were laid in Kure and Yokosuka, but the joy was instantly overridden by the crazier shipbuilding plan, the “Battleship M” featuring twelve 46cm guns. As a final answer to British, American, and German counterparts, the Navy Technical Department counted on it to secure Japan’s place in a race it could not win. Even though the accounting had pointed out that Japan could not afford such big ships, the plan still got seriously researched for strong support from the Navy General Staff.

By the end of 1924, the race had ended in practice by British withdrawal, and it was becoming not only an economic, but also a social problem that was dragging every participant into an abyss. The only lingering players at the table were Germany and the United States, who also were on the brink of economic crisis.

1925: The Epilogue

The British Revolution of 1925 ended the Second Naval Race officially. It did so by destroying the unified Royal Navy as the central reference point of global maritime policy. The race had been built around Britain’s effort to preserve supremacy and Germany’s effort to secure victory at sea, but now it became a global problem related to political and economic instability. Once Britain fractured, every naval program in the world required reinterpretation.

The old Admiralty tried to preserve continuity through evacuation, but found most of construction sites were occupied by anti-government workers. Canada became the main refuge because it possessed Atlantic ports, political loyalty to the Crown, and enough industrial depth to sustain at least part of the fleet, while the Union of Britain inherited the home islands and much of the physical naval infrastructure.

Germany reacted faster than any other continental power. The collapse of the old Royal Navy removed the immediate basis for some heavy-ship arguments, yet it opened a larger imperial problem. British colonies, concessions, and routes entered uncertainty. German diplomats, naval officers, and commercial firms pressed for advantage from the North Sea to Africa and East Asia. The Kaiserliche Marine now had to protect expanded responsibilities. The battle fleet remained important, while cruisers and auxiliaries gained new urgency. The navy’s focus changed from preventing British blockade to managing a world after British collapse. Two Elsaß-class battleships inched towards completion from 1925 to 1929, which they became the last witnesses of the era of craziness.

With one of its largest trade partners vanished, followed by the New York crash of April 1925, the United States experienced the shock through finance before it appeared through naval orders. It forced Congress and the Navy Department to reconsider every large program. The Louisiana-class battleships faced new scrutiny, most of which were cancelled, and only two of them survived but severely delayed. Some naval officers believed that a world without a stable Britain required stronger American independence at sea, but fiscal conservatives replied with financial state so harsh that foreign uncertainty was no longer enough to fund another battleship.

Japan, however, saw both relief and reluctance. The disappearance of a unified British Far Eastern presence, as well as pause of American programs, reduced some constraints on Japanese action before it got overwhelmed economically. The Navy Ministry completely lost its ground in the face of the spillover of an American economic crisis. The Finance Ministry, together with the Army, used the same crisis to push back costly naval projects, totally breaking the Navy’s funding from its source. The last two members of Kii and the aggressive “Battleship M” were abandoned before they could even begin, with Kii and Owari finished four years behind schedule. Doctrine became the cheaper form of ambition: torpedoes, night battle, submarines, cruisers, and carriers received greater attention as tools for defeating a stronger enemy without matching its entire battle line.

The Second Naval Race therefore closed without a formal settlement. No treaty, war, or official announcement proved it, but it was just over.

The revolution in Britain shattered the power whose decisions had driven much of the competition. The remaining powers entered the second half of the 1920s with different problems: Germany had to police victory with an even larger colonial empire, the United States had to deal with depression rather than global affairs, Japan had to seek decisive advantage within fiscal limits, and the two British navies had to fight over the inheritance of the world’s greatest maritime tradition.
